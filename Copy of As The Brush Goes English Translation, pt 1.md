# Preface

No matter how I think of myself, I am not a poet. I have never sat down with the intention of writing poetry, nor have I ever submitted poems to a magazine or newspaper contest. Nor is there any occasion on which I could say I made my debut as a poet. For that matter, even in the broader sense, I have no literary career. Looking back now, I remember writing something for *Yonsei Chunchu*, the newspaper of Yonsei University, but it was nothing that could be called a literary work, and I no longer remember it at all.

Then why have I decided to publish this small collection of writings as a book? To tell the truth, I am not entirely sure myself, but I suppose it is because of a lingering attachment to who I am. Having spent my entire life as a student and then as a university professor, always surrounded by books, I was fortunate enough to live immersed in the thoughts and emotions of many others.Without even realizing it, I too jotted down my own thoughts, feelings, and reflections from time to time.  
There was never any particular plan or purpose behind it. But now, as I find myself approaching the age of ninety, I began to think, *I should organize these, I should put them in order.* So I looked through my old notes, and today I have gathered them into this modest volume.

I simply wondered what it would be like. The writings included here are a collection of my thoughts and feelings, written down truly and unreservedly, just as the brush flowed.

I do hope that someone will read this. I hope that the reader will understand it positively and nod in agreement. While I do hope for my family and acquaintances to do so, it would also be fine if it were a complete stranger who happened to stop by a bookstore, bought it out of curiosity, and opened it.

I wonder if the writings and thoughts recorded in this book are like the seeds of wildflowers. Flowers, especially the countless wild flowers blooming on hillsides, produce an innumerable number of seeds and scatter them on the wind. Most simply fall somewhere and rot away. Fortunately, only a very small number will land in good soil, take root, grow, and bloom again. Some books are read by many and influence them the moment they are published, but there are countless others that remain unknown for decades, or even forever. Everything is bound to turn out this way, but even knowing this, we possess a desire to leave a record of the small movements of our hearts.

Since this is not writing that I produced systematically with the specific purpose of turning it into a book, the order of this book holds no particular significance. It was not written chronologically, nor is it grouped by content. It is simply...

I just did as I pleased. Feel free to flip through the pages as you please, or as you read...

It would be nice to put it down and read another page.

I initially intended to create a book containing only text, but I decided to include the paintings I have collected over the years. These are works by foreign artists who visited Korea from the opening of the Joseon Dynasty until the 1950s and captured our lives in paintings, including Keith, Miller, Jacoulet, Seiler, Hasui, and Yoshida. Most of them are woodblock prints and are part of my collection; they are interesting because they show us through the eyes of foreigners.

Two people contributed to the publication of my work—whether you call it poetry or prose—as a book. One is my friend Lee Dong-jun, an emeritus professor at Sungkyunkwan University, and the other is Ryu Jong-pil, the representative of Books & Together Publishing. Professor Lee had read through my writings and encouraged me to publish them whenever he had the chance. So I contacted Representative Ryu, and he replied promptly that he would publish them. It is thanks to the encouragement of these two people, who are far away across the Pacific Ocean, that I am writing this piece now.

# Father
**– On the way to the grave**

To me, having grown up as a   
boy, my father was a symbol of perfection—unchanging,   
unwavering, and flawless, like a finished   
sculpture to be emulated,   
or a Greek male figure seen in a museum.  
It even seemed a little cold.

To me, having become the father of growing children,   
my father was a strange traveler faded by the setting   
sun—just an incomprehensible shadow from the other side.

One day, suddenly and unexpectedly, to me who   
has become a grandfather, my father is approaching   
the countless versions of myself that have accumulated layer upon layer.

While sucking on his mother's breast, he began to love the soft body of a woman,  
I think I finally realize that all these boys, young men,   
and old men were my fathers—those old friends who used   
to play childishly outside the village until dusk, who   
would suddenly wander outside a woman's window and   
whistle, who still giggle and laugh even after turning sixty, and   
who still can't help but glance at the voluptuous breasts of women.

I confess that I did not know my father well.  
Just as my children do not know me completely.  
I admit that I was unable to have a serious conversation with my father all night long.  
Just as I don't have much of that experience or time with my children.

But when I think back  
Even though time and space were slightly different,   
we were turning two parallel wheels of life, learning from   
time to time with sidelong glances, and running toward the   
same place.
